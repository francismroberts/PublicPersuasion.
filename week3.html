Credibility

Be organized and sound organized. Be a person, not a drone. This builds authority and warmth — the two poles of trust.

Color

Vivid language, literary devices, rhetorical devices. This is what makes an already-credible speech memorable.

Weekly Big Ideas

Lecture · Reading

1. Choosing What You Want to Say Is a Tradition, Not a Task

Three steps from Lecture 1: it's not a task, it's a tradition (Aristotle: "only mankind was given the power of speech"); if you can't see it, you can't say it (visualize the room, the way Peggy Noonan pictured a family at the dinner table before writing for Reagan); then, and only then, choose your meaning. A speech happens at one shared moment in time — that's what makes it different from a memo.

Lecture

2. Credibility Is Built on Two Poles: Competence and Warmth

Aristotle's three appeals — logos, pathos, ethos — reduce to logic, emotion, and trust. Credibility specifically splits into competence/authority/strength on one side and likeability/humanity/warmth on the other. Rick Perry lost the 2012 Republican primary in one on-stage moment when he couldn't name a third federal agency; Tony Hayward lost his BP job for saying "I'd like my life back" during the Gulf oil spill. Different poles, same lesson: credibility can vanish in seconds.

Lecture · Reading

3. Most Persuasion Happens on the Peripheral Route

The Elaboration Likelihood Model (in the assigned reading) says audiences process messages two ways: central (careful, engaged, weighing evidence) or peripheral (fast mental shortcuts). Most real-world listening is peripheral — which is exactly why first impressions and credibility carry so much weight before anyone consciously evaluates your argument.

Lecture

4. Be Organized, Sound Organized, Be a Person

Concrete rules, not abstractions: use a roadmap/preview, transitions, and a review (Steve Jobs's "I want to tell you three stories" is the textbook example). Then write conversationally — shorter words, shorter sentences, turn dependent clauses into new sentences, flag key points with a colon, and lose the jargon. Organization builds authority; conversational tone builds warmth. Together, that's credibility.

Lecture · Reading

5. Color Is a Toolkit, Not a Talent

Vivid language (Churchill's "broad, sunlit uplands"), more words per square thought (Reagan's Boys of Pointe du Hoc), alliteration, metaphor (Cuomo's "tale of two cities" answering Reagan's "shining city on a hill"), tricolon, repetition (MLK's "let freedom ring"), quickened pace (FDR's Pearl Harbor address, Emma González's pause), rhetorical questions, rounded numbers, and paired lists — nine named, learnable techniques, each demonstrated in a real, famous speech.

Reading

6. Tone Is a Choice, Independent of Content

Cook's Chapter 9 adds a layer the lectures don't fully cover: six ways to control how words sound, regardless of what they say — terse and declarative (Cuomo), discursive (Georgie Anne Geyer), punchy with hard consonants (Malcolm X), soft and soothing (George H.W. Bush), breezy with contractions (Ann Richards), or formal and measured (Faulkner's Nobel acceptance, JFK's inaugural). The same idea can sound completely different depending on which register you choose.

Master Notes

1. Moment & Meaning: Choosing What You Want to Say

Core idea
A speech happens at one shared moment in time, which is what separates it from a memo — and that changes how you should write it.
Comes from
Lecture Part 1 · Reagan's Challenger address (Jan. 28, 1986)
Key details
Three steps: (1) it's not a task, it's a tradition — Aristotle's line that only mankind was given the power of speech; (2) if you can't see it, you can't say it — visualize the room, the way Peggy Noonan (in the packet, p. 3) pictured a child in a mall, or a family clearing dishes to watch an address; (3) choose your meaning — speeches don't live in silos of "informative" vs. "persuasive," so choose freely from the full range of what you could say. Case study: after the Challenger explosion killed all seven crew members live on television, Reagan's Oval Office address (written by Noonan, delivered ~12 hours later) had to make sense of the tragedy for five different audiences at once — schoolchildren, the public, the astronauts' families, NASA, and the Soviets — with one unifying message.
Why it matters
This is the mental model for approaching your own Persuasive Policy Speech: don't start with what to say — start with the room you'll be speaking into.

2. Building Credibility: Competence + Warmth

Core idea
Credibility (ethos) has two independent poles, and losing either one is fast and often fatal to a speaker.
Comes from
Lecture Part 2 · Reading pp. 1–12 (Aristotle, Elaboration Likelihood Model) · Steve Jobs at Stanford
Key details
Aristotle's Rhetoric: rhetoric is "the faculty of discovering the available means of persuasion in any given situation," with three appeals — logos (logic), pathos (emotion), ethos (credibility/trust). Competence/authority/strength is one pole; likeability/humanity/warmth is the other. Case studies: Rick Perry's 2012 debate "oops" moment (couldn't name the third federal agency he wanted to cut) cost him authority; BP CEO Tony Hayward's "I'd like my life back" cost him warmth. The Elaboration Likelihood Model explains why this matters so much: audiences mostly process peripherally (fast, instinctive judgments), not centrally (careful evaluation of arguments) — so credibility gets assessed almost instantly, often before the actual argument is heard.
Why it matters
The professor's claim: credibility is both the most important part of public speaking and the easiest to fix — because it comes down to two learnable habits, covered next.

3. Be Organized, Sound Organized, Be a Person

Core idea
Two concrete, teachable habits build both poles of credibility at once.
Comes from
Lecture Part 2 · Reading pp. 15–33 (organization + plain-English rules) · Steve Jobs at Stanford
Key details
Be organized: thesis + preview/roadmap ("today I want to tell you three stories" — Jobs), transitions/sign-posting, and a closing review. Sound organized = builds authority. Be a person: choose the more conversational word and the shorter sentence; turn dependent clauses into new sentences; flag key points conversationally (the "here's why it matters" colon trick); lose the jargon; read your draft out loud. Being a person = builds warmth. Both together are what the lecture calls "the foundation" — everything else (color, delivery) is built on top.
Why it matters
The professor's stated claim: with just these two habits, "we can usually make a better speechwriter in about 20 minutes" — this is the highest-leverage, fastest-to-apply material in the whole course.

4. Painting Pictures: Vivid Language and More Words Per Square Thought

Core idea
Once credibility is established, color starts with two techniques: choosing image-based words, and — counterintuitively — sometimes using more words, not fewer, to make one thought land.
Comes from
Lecture Part 3
Key details
Vivid language: words like "conquer," "battle," "desert," "oasis" — Churchill's radio-only "broad, sunlit uplands" and "abyss of a new Dark Age" created pictures with no visuals at all. More words per square thought: expand one simple idea for imagery (Reagan's "Boys of Pointe du Hoc" — the one-sentence fact "they took the cliffs" becomes a full paragraph of Rangers climbing rope ladders under fire) or for context/emphasis (restating "the test of leadership is persistence," or explaining what "34%" actually means to a room).
Why it matters
Both techniques exist for the same reason: to make sure a speech never needs a slide deck to be understood — the words themselves do the showing.

5. The Rhetorical Toolkit: Devices, Tone, and Rhythm

Core idea
A defined set of literary and rhetorical devices, each demonstrated in a specific, famous speech — plus six tone controls from the reading that operate independently of content.
Comes from
Lecture Parts 3–5 · Cook Ch. 9 · Mario Cuomo, Emma González, Jesse Jackson speeches
Key details
Devices: alliteration ("their blood was their bond"); metaphor/analogy (Kareem Abdul-Jabbar's racism-as-dust; Cuomo's "tale of two cities" and wagon-train answering Reagan's "shining city on a hill"; Richard Devine's extended Apollo 13 metaphor for restorative justice); tricolon/rule of three (Caesar's "I came, I saw, I conquered"; Lincoln's "we cannot dedicate..."); repetition (MLK's "let freedom ring"); quicken the pace (FDR's short, repeated "last night, Japanese forces attacked..."; Emma González's repeated "would never," ending in a held silence); rhetorical questions; rounded numbers (304,356 → "more than 300,000"); pairs instead of long lists. Tone (Cook Ch. 9): terse/declarative (Cuomo), discursive, punchy with hard consonants (Malcolm X), soft/soothing (George H.W. Bush), breezy with contractions (Ann Richards), formal (Faulkner, JFK's inaugural).
Why it matters
"These are like hats — you try them on to see what works for you." None of these are mandatory in every speech; the point is having a full toolkit to draw from once credibility is already secure.

6. Five Speeches, Five Master Classes

Core idea
Each assigned speech was chosen to demonstrate one specific part of this week's framework, not as a generic "great speeches" survey.
Comes from
All five assigned speeches, each paired with a specific lecture part
Key details
Reagan's Challenger address (Lecture 1) — choosing meaning for multiple audiences in one moment. Steve Jobs at Stanford (Lecture 2) — organization and conversational credibility. Mario Cuomo's 1984 DNC keynote (Lecture 3) — extended metaphor answering a rival speaker's imagery. Emma González (Lecture 4) — quickened pace and repetition building to a held silence. Jesse Jackson (Lecture 5) — assigned as an open exercise: spot the alliteration, metaphor, and repetition yourself, without the lecture pointing them out first.
Why it matters
Watching all five back-to-back at the end of the week (rather than only where each is first introduced) is a good way to hear the whole toolkit in five different real voices before Week 5's recap ties them together.
