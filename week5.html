How to Approach This Week

The final week, and the biggest deliverable of the term: the Persuasive Policy Speech, worth 70% of your grade across two parts. Everything this week is delivery — you've already done the writing (Weeks 3–4). The professor's framework is simple: conviction, connection, then the details. If the first two are real, the details tend to take care of themselves.

  1. Cook, Ch. 7 and the delivery lectures — read/watch before you record, not after.
  2. Persuasive Policy Speech — Part 1 (written script) — due Wednesday, Aug. 26. This is deliberately due before Part 2.
  3. Live Discussion Session — a good place to ask questions before the deadline.

"I never, ever deliver a totally blank, untouched text, hot off the printer." Plan your own time around that gap; don't write and record back-to-back.

Weekly Big Ideas

Lecture

1. Conviction, Connection, Details — In That Order

The same hierarchy from Week 2, applied to delivery: if you're genuinely committed to what you believe and to the people in the room, the "details" of good speaking — the things we hear and see — tend to take care of themselves. Very good speakers are often not the most polished ones; they're the ones with real conviction and connection.

Lecture

2. What We Hear: Tone, Volume, Rate, Pause, Emphasis

Rewatching Oprah's Golden Globes speech with eyes closed reveals it: a conversational baseline, punctuated by variation in volume and rate, real pauses (hold one beat longer than feels natural — a pause always feels longer to the speaker than the audience), and deliberate emphasis. "I didn't say she stole my book" said seven times, each with a different word stressed, produces seven different meanings — proof that emphasis is a choice, not an accident.

Lecture

3. What We See: Eye Contact, Face, Body, Gestures

Rewatching Mario Cuomo with the volume off isolates it: real eye contact (not "eye dart"), facial expression, physical space (research says more space read as more powerful), and natural gestures within the "power zone" — imagine holding a series of balls, from ping-pong to basketball, roughly from your midsection to your shoulders. Avoid the "death grip" on the lectern and the "wind in the willows" lean.

Lecture

4. Mark Up the Script — Never Deliver It Blank

Never staple your pages — sliding one page aside (the "DeSanto slide") beats flipping, which is distracting on camera. Practice paragraph by paragraph, marking where to speed up, slow down, or make an eye-contact note in the margin. Steve Jobs rented San Francisco's Moscone Center for a full week before his product launches purely to practice — polish is preparation, not talent.

Reading · Lecture

5. Nerves Are Universal — and Manageable

"I've been doing public speaking for about forty years... I have never, in my life, not been nervous." Cook's Chapter 7 explains why biologically: the "alarm reaction" (increased heart rate, lowered blood pressure, then a hormonal countershock) is automatic and normal. Concrete fixes: move deliberately, take deep breaths, avoid caffeine and dairy beforehand, and — most practically — over-prepare the opening, since that's where nerves peak hardest for everyone, including the professor.

Assignment

6. One Speech, Two Submissions, Any Topic You Choose

Part 1 (script) and Part 2 (video) share nearly identical instructions — same four strategies, same "audience of your choice," same requirement to name that audience on camera before you begin. The only real differences: Part 1 is 5–6 minutes as written; Part 2's delivered video runs 4–5 minutes, and Part 2 is worth slightly fewer points (30 vs. 40) since it's the same speech, delivered.

Master Notes

1. Conviction, Connection, Details — In That Order

Core idea
Delivery has a hierarchy, and the "details" people usually associate with public-speaking coaching are actually the lowest-leverage part of it.
Comes from
Lecture 2 Part 1 (opening framework)
Key details
Conviction: committed to what's in your heart. Connection: committed to everyone in the room. Details: everything people usually think of as "delivery skills" — tone, volume, gestures, eye contact. The professor's claim: when conviction and connection are real, the details tend to take care of themselves, and bad habits become much easier to coach out.
Why it matters
If your Part 2 delivery feels stiff in rehearsal, the fix usually isn't "try harder at gestures" — it's reconnecting with why you actually care about the topic you chose.

2. What We Hear: Tone, Volume, Rate, Pause, Emphasis

Core idea
Good vocal delivery is a conversational baseline punctuated by deliberate variation — "counterpoints" — not a uniformly polished performance.
Comes from
Lecture 2 Part 1 (Oprah Golden Globes clip, watched with eyes closed)
Key details
Base tone: conversational, the same "be a person" quality from Week 3. On top of that: volume changes (louder and softer, not just louder), rate changes (speeding up and slowing down — this is what most separates good speakers from great ones), and pauses (a pause always feels far longer to the speaker than to the audience — when you think you've paused long enough, hold one more beat). Emphasis is a conscious choice: "I didn't say she stole my book" delivered seven times, stressing a different word each time, produces seven distinct meanings. Writing the speech doesn't finish the job — you have to go back and make sure the emphasis actually lands on what you meant.
Why it matters
Practicing your Part 2 recording out loud (not just reading silently) is the only way to actually hear whether your emphasis matches your intended meaning.

3. What We See: Eye Contact, Face, Body, Gestures

Core idea
Visual delivery grows out of the same conviction and connection as vocal delivery, with a few specific, learnable techniques.
Comes from
Lecture 2 Parts 1–2 (Mario Cuomo clip, watched with volume off; live gesture demonstration)
Key details
Eye contact: real, held contact with individual people for a second or two each — not "public speaker eye dart" (mechanically scanning up and down). Face: facial expressions and willingness to smile show conviction and desire to connect. Body/space: research shows more physical space reads as more powerful — full body engagement, turning naturally toward different parts of the audience the way you would in any real conversation; avoid leaning ("wind in the willows") and keep shoulders level. Gestures: stay within the "power zone" (midsection to shoulders), imagine holding a graduated series of balls (ping-pong to basketball) for gesture size, keep hands above the lectern, and avoid the "death grip" on the lectern itself.
Why it matters
None of these are meant to be memorized as rules to perform — they're what naturally follows once you're actually trying to connect with a real audience, even a camera.

4. Mark Up the Script — Never Deliver It Blank

Core idea
Writing the speech and being ready to deliver it are two different jobs, and the gap between Part 1's and Part 2's due dates exists specifically to do the second one.
Comes from
Lecture 2 Parts 2–3
Key details
Never staple pages — sliding one page aside (the "DeSanto slide") avoids the distracting flip. Practice paragraph by paragraph, marking where to speed up, slow down, or add an eye-contact reminder in the margin. For a home setup with no real lectern, stacked boxes or books work fine — the point is simulating the physical situation, not the furniture. Steve Jobs rented the Moscone Center in San Francisco for a full week before major product launches purely to rehearse.
Why it matters
This is the direct, practical reason Part 1 (script) is due a full day before Part 2 (video) — use that day.

5. Nerves Are Universal — and Manageable

Core idea
Nervousness before speaking is a normal, automatic biological response, not a sign you're unprepared or bad at this.
Comes from
Lecture 2 Part 2 · Cook Ch. 7 (Alarm Reaction, Coping with the Shock-Adrenaline Response)
Key details
The professor, after roughly 40 years of public speaking, still gets nervous before every speech. Cook's Chapter 7 explains the biology: Hans Selye's "alarm reaction" — an initial shock phase (increased heart rate, lowered blood pressure) followed by a hormonal "countershock" that stabilizes the body, driven by adrenaline. Practical coping techniques: move deliberately if you feel rigid, take deep breaths before starting, avoid caffeine and dairy in the hours before (both affect the throat), and — most concretely — over-practice your opening specifically, since nerves peak hardest right at the start for nearly everyone.
Why it matters
Reframing nerves as normal (rather than a problem to eliminate) is itself part of the coping strategy — fighting the feeling of being nervous about being nervous just compounds it.

6. The Persuasive Policy Speech: What's Actually Being Asked

Core idea
One speech, submitted twice — a written script, then a video delivery of that same speech — on any topic you choose, for any audience you choose.
Comes from
Assignment instructions (Part 1 and Part 2, nearly identical text)
Key details
Topic: job-related, MSC-related, public policy, or otherwise — your choice, as long as it argues for a new policy, initiative, or belief. Audience: any you choose, but you must state who they are on the video before you begin speaking. Part 1: written in its entirety, 5–6 minutes as scripted, 40 points, due Wed. Aug. 26 by 5:00 p.m. CT. Part 2: video delivery of that same speech (minor live deviation from the script is fine), 4–5 minutes, 30 points, due Thu. Aug. 27 by 5:00 p.m. CT. Both list the same four strategies to apply: (1) visualize the audience and situation to choose your meaning; (2) be organized, use crisp conversational language and imagery; (3) use the persuasive tactics covered all term; (4) deliver with conviction and connection.
Why it matters
This is every concept from all five weeks, applied at once — notice the world (Week 1), find your conviction (Week 2), credibility and color (Week 3), logic and emotion (Week 4), and now delivery (Week 5).
