How to Approach This Week

Week 1 was about noticing that language has power. Week 2 is about finding out what you actually believe, before you're asked to persuade anyone of anything. Everything here builds toward one deliverable: a one-page summary of your own private writing, due Friday.

Lecture 1 opens with a question worth answering honestly before you read further: think of the best public speaker you've ever seen. What do you actually remember about them? Prof. DeSanto's answer, from years of asking that question, comes down to two words:

Conviction

Do you actually believe your message? Speakers with real passion and integrity read as believable, not like they're selling something.

Connection

Are you reaching the people in front of you — through story, relatability, feeling truly spoken to?

This week is entirely about the first pillar. Most of the rest of the course is about the second. You can't skip ahead to connection, because — as the $100 bill exercise in Lecture 2 puts it — you can't give what you don't have.

Weekly Big Ideas

Lecture

1. Persuasion Is Personal Advocacy for a Solution to a Problem

Lecture 1 reframes persuasion in three parts: something's broken (the problem), you have a fix (the solution), and you actually believe in it (the conviction). "I believe we should build a recycling program" — problem: pollution, solution: recycling. Skip any one piece and the persuasion collapses, no matter how polished the delivery.

Lecture · Reading

2. Conviction Matters More Now Than Ever

Not a platitude — Lecture 2 makes the case with numbers. A University of Chicago study found people less optimistic about the next generation than in 25 years, with reported isolation double what it was a few years ago. Meanwhile trust in traditional leaders keeps falling — the reading notes only 17% trusted Washington to do the right thing during the pandemic. A more confusing world with less-trusted leaders is exactly the environment where communicating from real conviction cuts through.

Lecture

3. Belief Drives Action — In You, and In Everyone You Address

Cognitive psychology's chain: beliefs lead to thoughts, thoughts lead to feelings, feelings lead to action. Whole Foods doesn't sell Oreos, because Whole Foods believes in fresh, organic food — and that communicated belief is what draws people through the door. After Steve Jobs died, people didn't leave notes at Apple Stores about the RAM in a MacBook; they wrote "you made me feel free." They were responding to what Apple stood for, not what it made.

Lecture

4. Private Writing Works Because It Combines Freedom and Discipline

Freedom: a dedicated space and time where you turn everything else off and let your right brain associate freely. Discipline: a timer and a page that keeps your mind from wandering off entirely. Mark Levy's Accidental Genius is the source for the whole method — write fast enough, and often enough, that ideas leave a trail instead of evaporating. Lee Iacocca's autobiography makes the same point about writing and accountability.

Lecture

5. Ten Named Techniques, One Underlying Process

Five basic moves (Try Easy, Write Fast and Continuously, Work Against a Limit, Write the Way You Think, Go with the Thought) generate raw material without self-editing. One refinement move (Redirect Your Attention) turns that raw material into your next prompt. Four advanced techniques (Open Up Words, Testing Assumptions, Nuggets of Wisdom, Paper Conversations) go after specific kinds of insight. All ten exist for one reason: get past what sounds impressive to what you actually think.

Reading · Live session

6. Conviction Doesn't Require Fame

Lecture 1 pairs Martin Luther King's "I Have a Dream" and Oprah Winfrey's Golden Globes speech with a talk almost nobody has heard of: a Georgia Tech student welcoming that year's freshman class. The pairing is deliberate — conviction isn't a function of who you are or how big the stage is. It's a function of whether you mean what you're saying.

Master Notes

1. Persuasion as Personal Advocacy: Problem, Solution, Conviction

Core idea
Persuasion is personal advocacy for a solution to a problem — three components, all required.
Comes from
Lecture 2 Part 1
Key details
Examples given: "I believe we should develop a recycling program" (problem: pollution, solution: recycling); "I believe we should change the department's vacation policy" (problem: morale, solution: policy change). The point isn't the topic — it's that all persuasive statements decompose into a broken thing, a fix, and a person who actually believes the fix matters.
Why it matters
This is literally the structure your Private Writing summary should reveal: what's broken (as you see it), what you're now considering, and why you care.

2. Why Conviction Matters More Than Ever

Core idea
Two forces make conviction unusually valuable right now: a more confusing world, and less-trusted traditional leaders.
Comes from
Lecture 2 Part 2 · Reading pp. 60–83 (Week 1 Packet)
Key details
25 years of disorienting events (impeachment, a Supreme-Court-decided election, 9/11, the Great Recession, fake news, a pandemic). A University of Chicago study found record-low optimism about the next generation and reported isolation double previous levels. The reading (p. 64) cites only 17% trust in Washington during the pandemic. Congress and the press poll at historic lows; non-traditional messengers (the lecture names Stephen Colbert, Jon Stewart) are trusted more than official ones.
Why it matters
Standing up as a leader is harder now precisely because leadership itself is trusted less — which is exactly why communicating from real, disclosed conviction stands out.

3. Belief Drives Action: From Oreos to Apple

Core idea
Belief precedes action, in a simple cognitive chain — and that's as true of audiences as it is of you.
Comes from
Lecture 2 Part 2
Key details
Belief → thought → feeling → action. Personal example: you won't exercise unless you believe it's worth doing. Organizational example: Whole Foods stocks zero Oreos because Oreos contradict what Whole Foods believes in (fresh, sustainable, organic) — and shoppers who share that belief show up because of it. After Steve Jobs's death, handwritten notes taped to Apple Store windows praised what Apple represented ("you made me feel free," "you helped me express my individuality"), not its products.
Why it matters
You can't persuade anyone of something you haven't first worked out you believe — the "$100 bill you don't have" problem from the lecture.

4. The Private Writing Method: Freedom + Discipline

Core idea
Private writing works by combining unstructured freedom of thought with the structural discipline of a timer and a page.
Comes from
Lecture 2 Part 2 · Lecture 2 Part 3 (How To) · Levy, Accidental Genius
Key details
Freedom: a dedicated time and space, everything else turned off, right-brain association allowed to wander. Discipline: a time limit and continuous writing so ideas leave a physical trail instead of getting lost. Source method is Mark Levy's Accidental Genius, tested across roughly 15 years of cohorts. Lee Iacocca's autobiography (in the packet) makes a parallel point: writing things down creates accountability to yourself.
Why it matters
This is the actual mechanism your private writing sessions rely on — understanding why it works makes it easier to trust the process during the workshop's fast, unpolished exercises.

5. Ten Techniques, One Process

Core idea
Five generative techniques, one refinement technique, and four advanced techniques — all aimed at getting past self-editing to what you actually think.
Comes from
Lecture 2 Part 3 (How To) · Private Writing Handouts
Key details
Basic (5): Try Easy (aim for 90%, not a breakthrough), Write Fast and Continuously (don't edit), Work Against a Limit (a timer keeps focus), Write the Way You Think ("friendly kitchen language," no explaining), Go with the Thought (improv "yes, and" — Levy's own example is marketing beef salami as a luxury product). Refinement (1): Redirect Your Attention — circle a question or interesting line from a prior writing and use it as the next prompt (Handout 2). Advanced (4): Open Up Words — redefine overused terms like "bandwidth" or "synergy" (Handout 3); Testing Assumptions — counter-intuitive what-ifs (Handout 4); Nuggets of Wisdom — agree or disagree with a quotation (Handout 5); Paper Conversations — a written dialogue with a real or imagined person (Handout 6).
Why it matters
You don't need to use all ten in your own sessions — the workshop is designed to help you find the one or two that actually generate something for you.

6. Three Faces of Conviction

Core idea
Conviction looks different depending on the speaker and the stage, but it's recognizable across all three registers.
Comes from
Speeches paired with Lecture 2 Part 1: MLK, Oprah Winfrey, Georgia Tech student speaker
Key details
MLK's "I Have a Dream" — the reference point for conviction at its most historically consequential. Oprah's Golden Globes remarks — conviction from someone "completely within herself, communicating from the inside out." A Georgia Tech upperclassman's freshman-convocation welcome — conviction from someone almost nobody in the audience had heard of, at an event with a tradition of being, in the lecture's own word, "boring."
Why it matters
The pairing is the argument: if conviction only mattered on the biggest stages, it wouldn't be worth five weeks of a course built around ordinary professional speaking.
