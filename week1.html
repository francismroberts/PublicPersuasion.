How to Approach This Week

Week 1 has one job: get you to notice how language already runs your life, before you spend four weeks learning to run it on purpose. Everything downstream — private writing, speechwriting, delivery — builds on the single idea both lectures return to: notice the world, then influence the world.

The Course Launch session (your first required item) is where Prof. DeSanto lays out how he organizes the whole term, not just this week. He frames the entire course around two pillars:

Conviction

The speaker's internal purpose — core values, active reflection, authentic passion, defining the problem you actually want to solve.

Connection

The audience's needs and values — audience analysis, clear organization, storytelling, conversational language, effective delivery.

The checklist below follows a specific order for a reason — you'll read the primary sources cold before either lecture interprets them for you:

Weekly Big Ideas

Live session · Lecture

1. Persuasion Is Power to Move Others, Not Authority Over Them

Perloff defines persuasion as "the study of attitudes and how to change them" — accurate, but incomplete. The course's working definition adds the missing piece: the ability to change others' beliefs and spur action. That's power, not authority — moving people who don't report to you, not just directing those who do.

Lecture

2. Words Govern Us and Words Inspire Us

Lecture 1 splits language into two registers: words that govern (contracts, marriage certificates, dress codes, performance reviews — language that creates rights, obligations, duties) and words that inspire (the Declaration of Independence, MLK's "Letter from a Birmingham Jail," a 2004 convention speech that turned a state senator into a future president in 17 minutes). Both prove words aren't decoration — they're the mechanism by which anything actually happens.

Lecture · Reading

3. The Mind Craves Order — and Panics Without It

Lecture 2 opens with Havel's idea that language is "social glue," and Borges's parable of Funes — a man who remembers everything perfectly but can't generalize or connect any of it, and goes mad. This week's reading supplies the real-world proof: the Psychology Today piece on conspiracy theories and the Slate piece on pandemic uncertainty both show people reaching for false certainty because the mind can't tolerate not knowing what comes next.

Lecture · Reading

4. Meaning Gets Manufactured, Not Just Found

"Haagen-Dazs" means nothing in any language — Reuben Mattus invented it, umlaut included, because it sounded luxurious. Ben & Jerry's raised their first $4,000 on a business plan — words describing a vision, not yet a business. And when Google went public, its founders turned a routine SEC filing into "An Owner's Manual for Google's Shareholders," complete with "Don't Be Evil." Language doesn't just describe meaning — it creates it, on purpose.

Reading

5. 272 Words Decided What a War Meant

By Gettysburg, Lincoln's war — once predicted to last three weeks — was in its third year; 40,000 soldiers were dead or wounded from that battle alone, and he had a re-election to weigh in a critical state. He didn't resolve any of that with policy. He stood up and, in under three minutes and 272 words, decided what the battle, the war, and the country would mean going forward. That's the ceiling for what words can do.

Reading · Live session

6. Sense-Making Is a Leadership Skill, Not Just a Lecture Topic

The HBR excerpt in this week's reading names sense-making and visioning as core skills separating great leaders from the rest — diagnosing what's broken, then describing what could exist instead. That's this week's abstract idea (order from chaos) landing as a concrete, resume-relevant skill — and it's exactly what the Course Launch's "Conviction" pillar is asking you to start building.

Master Notes

1. Defining Persuasion: From Attitudes to Action

Core idea
Two working definitions of persuasion — and the course deliberately picks the more demanding one.
Comes from
Course Launch (live session) · Lecture 1
Key details
Perloff: persuasion is the study of attitudes and how to change them. This course's definition: the ability to change others' beliefs and spur action — action is the missing piece. "Public" in the course title means addressing groups, not one-on-one conversation. Power (moving people who don't answer to you) is distinguished sharply from authority (people who do).
Why it matters
Every assignment this term is graded on whether it moves someone to actually do something, not just agree with you in the abstract.

2. Words That Govern vs. Words That Inspire

Core idea
Language operates in two registers, and most of it hides in plain sight because it's so constant.
Comes from
Lecture 1
Key details
Governing words: contracts, marriage certificates, dress codes, performance evaluations — language creating rights, obligations, duties, often without you registering it as language at all. Inspiring words: the Talmud, the Koran, the Gospels, the Declaration of Independence, MLK's "Letter from a Birmingham Jail." Modern example: Obama's 2004 DNC speech, given while he was one of 59 Illinois state senators, put him on a track to the presidency four years later.
Why it matters
You already read and produce governing language daily without registering its power; this course is about learning to wield the inspiring register on purpose.

3. The Human Need for Order (Havel & Borges)

Core idea
Language exists to satisfy a basic human need: making sense of the world and imposing order on chaos.
Comes from
Lecture 2 (Havel, Borges) · Reading pp. 28–33 (Psychology Today, Slate)
Key details
Havel: language is "social glue" — it unites disparate facts and answers "why" and "what next." Borges's Funes remembers every experience perfectly but can't generalize between any two of them, and the inability to make sense of anything drives him mad. The assigned reading supplies the real evidence: Psychology Today explains conspiracy-theory belief as a search for certainty in a confusing world; Slate makes the same case about pandemic-era anxiety over not knowing when things would end.
Why it matters
If people crave order this badly, providing it — clearly, credibly — is itself a persuasive act. This is the psychological mechanism the rest of the course is built on.

4. Meaning Manufactured: Ice Cream and Google

Core idea
Words don't just describe existing meaning — they can manufacture meaning where there was none.
Comes from
Lecture 2 (Haagen-Dazs, Ben & Jerry's) · Reading pp. 40–59 (Google S-1)
Key details
"Haagen-Dazs" is a made-up word — no meaning in any language — chosen by Reuben Mattus purely for its luxurious sound. Ben & Jerry's raised their first $4,000 on a business plan: a story told in words about a business that didn't exist yet. Google's actual SEC Form S-1 (in your assigned reading) opens with "An Owner's Manual for Google's Shareholders" and "Don't Be Evil" — turning a routine legal filing into a statement of identity investors could buy into.
Why it matters
Direct bridge to Private Writing next week: you're not just describing a policy problem, you're building the language that makes people believe the problem — and your solution — mean something.

5. The Gettysburg Address: 272 Words, One New Meaning

Core idea
The clearest proof in the course that language can permanently redefine what an event means.
Comes from
Reading pp. 1–16 (the speech text itself) · Lecture 2 (context)
Key details
Delivered in 1863, three years into a war originally predicted to last three weeks; roughly 40,000 casualties at Gettysburg alone, ~700,000 dead across the whole war; Lincoln had his own re-election to weigh, in a critical state. Under three minutes, 272 words. After the speech, the battle, the war, the Constitution, and the country meant what Lincoln said they meant.
Why it matters
This is the ceiling the course is aiming you toward — not for length or occasion, but for what's possible when conviction and word choice align.

6. Sense-Making, Visioning, and the Road Ahead

Core idea
This week's abstract idea (language creates order) is also a named, teachable leadership skill — and a preview of the whole course.
Comes from
Reading pp. 40–59 (HBR excerpt) · Course Launch (Conviction/Connection framework)
Key details
HBR names sense-making (diagnosing what's broken) and visioning (describing what could exist instead) as core leadership capabilities. The Course Launch session frames the whole term the same way: Conviction (internal purpose — core values, the problem you want to solve) and Connection (audience needs and values — analysis, organization, storytelling). The professor's own roadmap: private writing, then speechwriting, then presentation delivery.
Why it matters
Everything from here through Week 5 is this week's idea, applied: find your conviction (Week 2), connect it to an audience (Weeks 3–4), deliver it (Week 5).
