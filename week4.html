How to Approach This Week

Three true paths to influence: credibility (Week 3), logic, and emotion. This week is the last two. The professor's closing framework for the whole term is Know, Feel, Do — know is logic, feel is emotion, do is credibility/trust. Every part of your speech should be checked against all three.

The week has a clear arc: logic first, then its limits, then emotion picks up exactly where logic runs out.

  1. Week 3 Packet, pp. 1–35 — fallacies, two-sided messaging, and evidence effectiveness, cited by exact page range in Lecture 1.
  2. Lecture 1 (Using Logical Appeals) — facts, organization, strong openings, then the honest limits of logic.
  3. Week 3 Packet, pp. 55–117 (three ranges) — supporting material for the emotional-appeals lectures.
  4. Lecture 1 Part 1 (Using Emotional Appeals) and Part 2 — stories, values, shared history, urgency, and a fully annotated Obama speech tying everything together.
  5. Luntz, Ch. 1–4, 7–8, 10, 12 — read alongside or after the lectures; Ch. 1's Ten Rules is the most concrete material in the book, and Ch. 7–8's case studies (Jack Welch, the "death tax," the Contract with America) show the rules applied for real stakes.
  6. Cook, Ch. 10 — a genuinely different angle (source credibility research, cognitive dissonance, body-language studies) worth reading after both lectures.
Luntz, chapter by chapter Ch. 1 — the Ten Rules (simplicity, brevity, credibility, consistency, and six more). Weekly Big Ideas

Lecture

1. Three True Paths, One Framework: Know, Feel, Do

Aristotle's logos, pathos, and ethos translate into a troubleshooting question for any part of a speech: what does my audience need to know (logic)? How do they need to feel (emotion)? Have I done enough for them to do what I'm asking (credibility/trust)? The professor's closing line: "the leader follows, though a step ahead" — real leadership in a speech means pulling people forward from where they already are, not where you wish they were.

Lecture

2. Using Logic: Facts, Organization, and Strong Openings

Facts and numbers need "more words per square thought" to land (a callback to Week 3). Structure follows problem-solution, often in three parts. Openings should grab attention before the roadmap — a stunning unrounded number (430,222) or a pop-culture reference, not "thank you for having me." Two-sided messaging (the "sandwich": positive argument, brief counterargument, more positive argument) builds credibility precisely because it doesn't hide the other side.

Lecture

3. The Limits of Logic: Fallacies and Motivated Reasoning

Avoid questionable authority, either-or framing, inconsistency, ad hominem, slippery slope, and hasty conclusions. But the deeper problem: a famous Stanford study gave two groups fake performance scores, then told both groups the scores were fabricated — people still rated their own abilities based on the fake numbers. Impressions, once formed, are remarkably resilient, especially when audiences are "ego-involved" — a Dartmouth-Princeton study found fans from each school watched the identical football film and each blamed the other team for dirty play.

Lecture

4. Using Emotion: Stories That Move People

A story is defined precisely: "an imagined or reimagined experience narrated with enough detail and feeling to cause your listener's imagination to experience it as a real sensation." Three kinds: example (something that actually happened), vision (painting what the world could look like), and mission (giving the audience a stake in something larger). A viral clip about a widower who painted his whole Ohio town after losing his wife does more than any statistic about civic engagement ever could.

Lecture · Reading

5. Values, History, Urgency, and the Power of We

Frame the problem as inconsistent with shared values, and the solution as consistent with them — a capuchin monkey refusing a cucumber once it sees its neighbor getting a grape shows fairness is practically biological. Lincoln's "four score and seven years ago" is a historical argument: appeal to what we already agree on. Luntz's polling makes the same point with numbers: by nearly two-to-one, Americans say we spend too much on "welfare" — but flip to "assistance to the poor," describing the identical programs, and 68% say we spend too little. Close with urgency (why now), optimism (the world as it can be), and unity — the deliberate, repeated use of "we."

Reading

6. Credibility Under the Microscope: Source, Argument, Body

Cook's Chapter 10 adds real research the lectures don't fully cover: source credibility depends on reputation, fairness, and even how well your intro is delivered (Boeing's president publicly admitting fault after a pricing scandal actually rebuilt trust). Cognitive dissonance can be used deliberately — showing an audience two beliefs they hold that can't both be true. And Dartmouth researchers found presidential debate winners across three different elections shared strikingly similar body language, regardless of their arguments' content.

Master Notes

1. Three True Paths, One Framework: Know, Feel, Do

Core idea
Logos, pathos, and ethos reduce to a simple diagnostic: know (logic), feel (emotion), do (credibility/trust) — trust is what makes an audience willing to act at all.
Comes from
Emotional Appeals Part 2 (closing synthesis)
Key details
The framework is explicitly a troubleshooting tool: at each part of a speech, ask whether you've given logical thinkers enough facts, given emotionally-motivated listeners enough feeling, and built enough trust (organization + conversational tone, from Week 3) that people will actually act on what you're asking. Research becomes important here — not just to find facts, but to find the right facts and the right stories for a specific audience.
Why it matters
This is the single most useful editing checklist for your own Persuasive Policy Speech draft — run every section through Know/Feel/Do before you consider it finished.

2. Using Logic: Facts, Organization, and Strong Openings

Core idea
Logic in a speech isn't just correct facts — it's facts delivered with enough context to land, inside a structure the audience can follow without effort, in words plain enough to actually be understood.
Comes from
Lecture 1 (Using Logical Appeals) · Reading pp. 1–35 (two-sided messaging, pp. 10–13) · Luntz Ch. 1 (Ten Rules)
Key details
Numbers need "more words per square thought" — set up, deliver, reinforce. Problem-solution is the default structure, often in three parts (rule of three again). Openings should gain attention before the roadmap: an unrounded "stunning number" (430,222 — the opposite of Week 3's rounding advice) or a pop-culture reference, deliberately "playing against type" instead of a generic thank-you. Two-sided messaging — stating the opposing argument briefly, then countering it, sandwiched between positive points — builds credibility precisely because it signals you aren't hiding anything. Luntz's first two rules back this up directly: Simplicity ("avoid words that might force someone to reach for the dictionary") and Brevity ("never use a sentence when a phrase will do") — Lincoln wrote 76% of the Gettysburg Address in words of five letters or fewer.
Why it matters
Two-sided messaging is a direct, practical tool for the "opposing viewpoints" section any strong policy speech needs — and simple, short language is what makes sure your logic actually lands instead of sailing over an audience's head.

3. The Limits of Logic: Fallacies and Motivated Reasoning

Core idea
Avoiding fallacious reasoning matters, but the bigger problem is that facts alone often can't move an audience whose identity is bound up in a position.
Comes from
Lecture 1 (Using Logical Appeals) · Reading pp. 1–35 (fallacies pp. 3–8, evidence pp. 13–24) · Cook Ch. 10 (philosophers on persuasion)
Key details
Common fallacies to avoid: questionable authority, either-or framing, inconsistency, ad hominem, slippery slope, hasty conclusion. A famous 1970s Stanford study told two groups their scores on a task were real, then revealed the scores were fabricated — participants' self-assessments stayed anchored to the fake numbers anyway; "once formed, impressions are remarkably resilient." A Dartmouth-Princeton study found fans of each school, watching the identical football film, each identified the other team as playing dirty — a phenomenon researchers call "ego-involvement" or "motivated reasoning," explained by theories like belief in a just world, system justification, social dominance orientation, and cultural cognition. Cook's Chapter 10 adds a historical footnote: for 1,500 years, scientists believed arrows stayed aloft because of air rushing in behind them, purely because Aristotle said so.
Why it matters
This is the most important limitation to understand in the whole course: no matter how good your fact is, it may simply not reach an audience that's identity-invested in disagreeing — which is exactly why emotion isn't a lesser tool, it's the necessary one.

4. Using Emotion: Stories That Move People

Core idea
Stories connect people at one shared moment the way pure data cannot, and come in three distinct types depending on the goal.
Comes from
Emotional Appeals Part 1
Key details
Definition: "an imagined or reimagined experience narrated with enough detail and feeling to cause your listener's imagination to experience it as a real sensation." Example story — something that actually happened (a specific customer journey). Vision story — paints what the world could look like for someone affected by your idea, even without a real named person. Mission story — gives the audience a stake in something larger than themselves, often tied to shared history. Illustrative case: a viral news segment about a widower in Glouster, Ohio who repainted his whole town after his wife's death — impressive shuttle statistics were shown right after as a deliberate contrast, to demonstrate that raw numbers don't move people the way a story does.
Why it matters
A story is the most direct route to the "feel" part of Know/Feel/Do, and Steve Jobs's Stanford speech (Week 3) was, in the professor's own framing, built entirely out of three stories.

5. Values, History, Urgency, and the Power of We

Core idea
Frame the problem as inconsistent with values the audience already holds, and the solution as consistent with them — then add urgency, optimism, and unity to move from belief to action.
Comes from
Emotional Appeals Part 1 · Cook Ch. 10 (emotional-appeal categories) · Luntz Ch. 2, 8, 10 (reframing research)
Key details
Values run deep: a famous fairness study gave capuchin monkeys cucumber for a task, which they accepted happily — until they saw a neighboring monkey get a grape for the same task, after which they refused the cucumber outright. A Subaru ad about "the humble back seat" never mentions specs — it sells love, family, and safety. Shared history works the same way Lincoln used it at Gettysburg ("four score and seven years ago") and in the Lincoln-Douglas debates over what the founders intended for slavery — both sides argued from the same shared past. Close with urgency (why this matters right now), an optimistic vision of what's possible, and deliberate, repeated use of "we" (a unity appeal). Luntz's polling makes the framing mechanism explicit: the identical government programs poll at 42% "too much spending" under the label "welfare," but only 7% "too much spending" under "assistance to the poor." The 2001 campaign to repeal the "estate tax" stalled for years; renamed the "death tax," it passed. Even the order information arrives in matters — Luntz found reversing the sequence of three identical campaign videos completely flipped how a focus group judged the same candidate.
Why it matters
Cook's Chapter 10 adds a usable list of common emotional appeals beyond values alone — justice/fair play, patriotism, sympathy, desire for freedom, guilt, conformity, and fear — with an explicit ethical line: appeals to greed, or to fears that don't correspond to real risk, are widely considered out of bounds. Luntz's research adds the practical discipline: the same underlying policy, described in different words, can poll as popular or unpopular — so word choice isn't decoration on top of your argument, it's part of the argument.

6. Credibility Under the Microscope: Source, Argument, Body

Core idea
Cook Ch. 10 organizes decades of persuasion research into three factors: source credibility, effective argumentation, and body language — each with specific, actionable findings.
Comes from
Cook Ch. 10 (reading only — not covered directly in the lectures)
Key details
Source credibility: reputation (Boeing's president publicly admitted fault after a fraud scandal — direct admission rebuilt more trust than denial had cost); belief/attitude similarity (Gerald Ford's 1976 debate claim that Poland wasn't under Soviet control tanked his credibility instantly); fairness (a land developer's claim a new store would be "owner-operated" collapsed under questioning about what "owner" actually meant). Effective argumentation: people are more loss-averse than gain-motivated; reframe issues rather than attack them head-on; cognitive dissonance can be used deliberately by pointing out two beliefs an audience holds that can't both be true; give your best support first and last (the primacy/recency effect). Body language: Dartmouth researchers analyzing the 1960, 1976, and 1980 presidential debates found winners shared relaxed, warm, good-humored styles regardless of their specific arguments — Kennedy over Nixon, Carter over Ford, Reagan over Carter.
Why it matters
This chapter is the single deepest research grounding for credibility in the whole course — worth returning to specifically when preparing to deliver (not just write) your speech in Week 5.
